The model

I treat each particle as a hard sphere, with gaussian blur in 3D. Because of the lower z-resolution of the confocal microscopy, the blur is anisotropic. For particle i, I can write down its contribution (Si) to the the image as,

Si=Ii⋅(Pi∗G)

where I is the intensity of the spehere, P is a “Platonic” particle without blur, and G is the gaussian kernel. A good algorithm to simulate the Platonic particle can be found in the SI of this nice PRX paper about particle tracking. As a result, the model of a confocal image (M) can be written as

M=∑iSi.

In a more operational way, I can simulat a confocal image, knowing a collection of particle locations, the radii of these particles and their intensitiy values, following the pseudo-code,

for i in range(n_particles):
  position = positions[i]
  intensity = intensities[i]
  radius = radii[i]
  P = generate_single_sphere(radius)
  box = get_sphere_container(positoin)
  M[box] += P * intensity
apply_kernel(M, G)

The Derivative of the Model

If we are comparing the model with the experimental data, E, we can take the squared difference as a measure of cost (C). A good model should then be the model that minimise the difference, written as,

C=∑x∑y∑z(M−E)2⋅W=∑x∑y∑z⏟sum over all voxelsD2⋅W

where D is the difference between the experimental image and simulation, and W is the weight for different voxels. I choose W=1 and treat all pixel equally. Another option would be setting W∝E to stress the importance of bright voxels.

Suppose we want to use the criteria to find the particle locations, using algorithms like gradient descent, then we will need the derivative of C w.r.t. particle locations. The derivative w.r.t. the x component of particle i can be written as

∂C∂xi=∑x∑y∑zW⋅∂D2∂xi=∑x∑y∑z2DW⋅∂D∂xi=∑x∑y∑z2DW⋅∂[∑jIj⋅(Pj∗G)−E]∂xi.

We know the experimental image would not be affected by the location of particle i. Therefore, ∂E/∂xi=0, and the derivative is finally,

∂C∂xi=∑x∑y∑z2DW⋅∂∑j[Ij⋅(Pj∗G)]∂xi=∑x∑y∑z2DW⋅∑j(∂[Ij⋅(Pj∗G)]∂xi)=∑x∑y∑z2DW⋅∑j(∂Ij∂xi(Pj∗G)+Ij∂(Pj∗G)∂xi)

Exploiting the property of convolution operator

ddx(f∗g)=f∗dgdx,

we can further rewrite the differentiation as

∂C∂xi=∑x∑y∑z2DW⋅∑j(∂Ij∂xi(Pj∗G)+Ij⋅(Pj∗∂G∂xi))

since the intensity (Ij) and shape (Pj) of particle j would not be affected by the location of particle i, the derivative can be simplified to the following,

∂C∂xi=∑x∑y∑z2DW(∂Ii∂xi⋅(Pi∗G)+Ii⋅(Pi∗∂G∂xi))

This equation can be used to evalulate the Jacobian matrix for the least–square fitting. It will be very fast because we only have to conserder a small sub–image, where Pi and its blurred version is non–zero. Formally, we can write something like,

∂C∂xi=∑x′∑y′∑z′⏟where Pi∗G>02DW(∂Ii∂xi⋅(Pi∗G)+Ii⋅(Pi∗∂G∂xi)).

And the new summation range is much smaller.

Numerical Implementation

I implemented the cost function in my tracking code, and use the least square optimisation to update the particle locations.
